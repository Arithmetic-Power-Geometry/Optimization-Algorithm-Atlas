# Academic Writing Standard

## Register
Use formal, technically precise, discipline-appropriate language. Prefer explicit mechanisms, conditions and evidence over promotional adjectives.

## Preferred terminology
Use:
- "the evidence indicates/suggests" rather than "clearly proves" for empirical synthesis;
- "under the evaluated conditions" rather than universal superiority;
- "mechanistically similar/distinct" rather than "completely new";
- "evidence gap" rather than "research gap" when absence of adequate evidence is the actual finding;
- "documented limitation" only when directly supported;
- "untested condition" when evidence is absent;
- "failure frontier" only for a predeclared, empirically estimated transition;
- "canonical representative" rather than "best algorithm";
- "comparative evidence" rather than "winner";
- "computational overhead" and "objective-evaluation cost" separately.

Avoid:
- revolutionary, groundbreaking, unprecedented, ultimate, perfect, universally best;
- unsupported "first", "novel", "state-of-the-art";
- anthropomorphic/metaphorical explanation when a computational description is possible;
- citation stacking without explaining what each source establishes.

## Claim grammar
### Literature fact
"Smith et al. reported X under Y conditions."

### Review synthesis
"Across the included studies, evidence for X is concentrated in Y, whereas Z remains weakly characterized."

### Contradictory evidence
"Reported outcomes are inconsistent across studies that differ in A, B and C; the available evidence therefore does not support a condition-independent conclusion."

### Repository-generated result
"Under the predeclared experimental protocol, the observed transition occurred at ..."

### Gap
"No adequate evidence was identified for X under the declared search protocol; this is classified as UNTESTED rather than as a failure."

## Paragraph architecture
Each analytical paragraph should normally follow:
1. proposition;
2. supporting evidence;
3. boundary/qualification;
4. synthesis or implication.

## Section architecture
Each major section should answer a named research question and end with a synthesis that motivates the next section.

## Numerical language
Report magnitude, uncertainty, condition and denominator. Avoid isolated percentages and rank-only interpretation.

## Tense
Use past tense for individual published experiments; present tense for established definitions and the paper's own structure; use cautious present/present-perfect for synthesis.

## Acronyms
Define at first use. Avoid introducing acronyms used fewer than three times unless standard in the field.

## Human readability
Prefer short technical sentences when notation is dense. Avoid excessive nominalization, repeated "Furthermore/Moreover", generic transition phrases, and formulaic literature-summary prose.
