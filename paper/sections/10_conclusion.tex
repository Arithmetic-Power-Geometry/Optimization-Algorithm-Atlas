\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study treats optimization research as a problem of connecting names, mechanisms, evidence, and claims without erasing the conditions under which each result was obtained. A 90-entry cross-paradigm census provides the identity layer, while 42 source-backed fingerprints provide a representative mechanism layer. These counts serve different purposes: breadth of coverage is not equated with the number of genuinely distinct computational principles.

The mechanism-first view organizes methods by the state they preserve, the information they exploit, the geometry by which they generate proposals, and the memory, selection, adaptation, constraint, diversity, and model structures that shape search. This representation makes it possible to compare methods drawn from otherwise separate traditions without reducing them to historical labels or metaphors.

The evidence model adds a second discipline. Documented, partial, contradictory, untested, and project-validated conditions are kept distinct, so lack of study is not mistaken for failure and structural similarity does not automatically transfer empirical conclusions between algorithms. Theory is similarly retained with its assumptions and guaranteed object rather than collapsed into a generic claim of convergence.

The computational study provides the executable counterpart. Random Search, differential evolution, Nelder--Mead, and CMA-ES were evaluated on all 24 noiseless BBOB functions across dimensions 5, 10, 20, and 40 under normalized evaluation budgets. The discovery and held-out campaigns each produced 14,400 validated checkpoint rows and 64 algorithm-labelled COCO roots, followed by official \texttt{cocopp} processing. The paired ECDF views show substantial dependence on target difficulty and problem class rather than a stable condition-independent ordering.

The held-out campaign also illustrates why experimental success and claim strength are not the same thing. Because the exact numerical reliability threshold and target subset required for a confirmatory binary failure frontier were not fully frozen before held-out evaluation, no post hoc frontier is reported. Likewise, the evidence did not justify a mechanism-level repair opportunity, so no new optimizer was introduced merely because one could have been designed.

The resulting contribution is therefore methodological and empirical rather than promotional. It provides a reproducible path from problem condition to mechanism, evidence state, experiment, and claim. That path can support future theory, benchmarking, algorithm selection, or mechanism-repair studies while making explicit what evidence is still missing.

Optimization research benefits when uncertainty is made visible rather than hidden by aggregate rankings or new names. In many cases the most useful next contribution is not another optimizer, but a sharper experiment that determines which mechanism matters, under which conditions, and with what level of evidence.
